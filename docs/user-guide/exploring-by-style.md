# Exploring by style

Open **Style & movement** in the Explore filters. Styles are grouped into six families:

| Family | Styles |
|---|---|
| European traditions | Gothic, Early / High / Northern Renaissance, Mannerism, Baroque, Rococo, Neoclassicism, Romanticism, Realism, Pre-Raphaelite, Impressionism, Post-Impressionism, Symbolism, Art Nouveau |
| Modern movements | Fauvism, Expressionism, Cubism, Futurism, Dada, Constructivism & Bauhaus, Surrealism, Abstract Expressionism, Pop Art, Minimalism |
| Asian traditions | Chinese literati painting, Shan shui landscape, Kanō & Rinpa schools, Ukiyo-e, Nihonga, Persian miniature, Mughal painting, Rajput & Pahari painting, Bengal School, Gutai |
| African & diasporic | Ancient Egyptian, West & Central African sculpture, Kingdom of Benin court art, Harlem Renaissance, African modernism |
| The Americas | Pre-Columbian, Hudson River School, Mexican Muralism, Brazilian Modernismo |
| Contemporary | Contemporary art |

"Modern art" is split into its individual movements, so you can tell Cubism from Surrealism.

## How to use it

- Click a style chip to add it, and click it again to remove it. You can pick several; results include any of them.
- **Hover over** (or long-press) a chip for a one-line description.
- Type in **Find a style…** to filter the list.

## Smart suggestions

When you've chosen a region or an era, the style list adapts:
- Styles that fit your choice move to the top.
- Styles from a different time or place are **greyed out**, and their tooltip says why, for example "Active 1907 – 1925, outside your era". You can still select them, which is useful for spotting cross-cultural influences.
- Switch on **Only styles matching my era and region** to hide the greyed-out ones.

## Good to know

- Some museums tag works with a style; for others the site matches the style's name in the work's description. Results for well-documented movements, such as Impressionism, are more precise than for broad traditions.
- Wikidata contributes many works for movements such as Romanticism, the Harlem Renaissance and Nihonga.
